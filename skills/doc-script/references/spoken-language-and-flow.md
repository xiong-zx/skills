# Write for speaking and listening

## Recover a voice the author can use

Use the requested speaking language and the author's accepted phrasing or spoken sample when available. A sample helps but is not a prerequisite. If the talk is in English and the chat is Chinese, keep the spoken passage in English and put requested explanations outside it.

Prefer direct subjects, familiar verbs and complete connected sentences. English contractions such as "we'll", "it's" and "doesn't" can make a research talk easier to say; use them where they fit the speaker, not at a quota. Break dense subordinate clauses and noun chains into breath-sized thought units without turning the passage into disconnected fragments. Keep a necessary scientific term, explain it where needed and reuse the same name.

Avoid essay openings, ceremonial wording, abstract inventories, self-praise and announcing every slide title. Oral delivery can remain precise and professional. Do not add filler, forced humor, personal anecdotes, slang or promotional excitement to manufacture a human voice. Preserve the author's I/we and attribution; do not turn a cited result into the speaker's own work.

## Build real transitions

For each page, privately identify what the audience now knows, what is still missing and what this page adds. Give the listener enough of that connection to stay oriented. Within the page, make the actual relation clear: reason, consequence, contrast, example, limitation or sequence.

A transition should carry information. It can connect an observation to a question, a limitation to a proposed idea, a definition to why it matters, or a result to its interpretation. Do not mechanically begin every page with "Now" or "So", force a question-and-answer structure, or insert a transition sentence at a fixed location.

Changing topics is legitimate. Signal a shift or a separate case without inventing causality or a mandatory research storyline. If a scientific link is missing, flag it; fluent wording cannot supply the proof. When the deck's order makes the explanation awkward, repair the spoken bridge if faithful, and suggest a deck change separately within the user's scope.

Use selective reminders of an important object or result when they prevent loss of context. Repeating an essential term is useful; repeating the full slide and then summarizing it again usually wastes attention. Check the beginning and end carefully, but do not require a dramatic opening, roadmap or motivational ending for every occasion.

## Deliver prose, with cues kept distinct

Use slide number plus title or another verified locator as navigation, followed by connected spoken paragraphs. Headings are not spoken. For a continuous-speech request, keep page boundaries unobtrusive.

Put short delivery cues in a clearly marked non-spoken block, such as "Delivery cues — not spoken: point to the dashed curve; pause after the comparison". Keep longer technical reminders, Q&A material and citations separate from the spoken passage. Do not turn the script into only bullets or cues unless the user asked for that format.

An essential qualifier belongs in the audible explanation, even if a formal version also appears in notes. No silent cue may be the only place that restricts an otherwise misleading spoken claim.

## Before and after

These are constructed examples of oral phrasing. They are not findings from the user's project, and may only be reused when their facts and logical relations fit the actual source.

**Disconnected facts and a slide announcement**

Before: "We compare two estimators. One uses fresh samples. The other reuses samples. The results are on the next slide."

After: "The two estimators use the data differently: one draws fresh samples, while the other reuses samples it already has. That changes the cost of each update. On the next slide, let's compare them under the same total sampling budget."

This presumes the source actually studies that budget. Do not change a computation-time comparison into a sample-budget comparison to use this wording.

**A formal sentence that is hard to say**

Before: "The consideration of dependence between observations necessitates a modification of the analysis."

After: "The observations can depend on one another. That means we can't reuse the independence argument from before, so we need to change this part of the analysis."

Use only if the preceding argument really relied on independence. Do not invent a prior argument or claim that all existing analyses assume it.

**A conclusion with its limitation still audible**

Before: "The experiments demonstrate the superiority of the proposed method."

After: "In these experiments, our method reaches a lower error at the same sampling budget. That's encouraging, but we haven't checked whether the advantage holds outside these settings."

This is appropriate only when the inspected results support the named metric and budget. Replace the invented comparison or uncertainty with the source's actual one; do not quietly repair an unsupported claim through style.

The operational rules adapt [Stanford's written-to-spoken guidance and delivery advice](sources.md), MIT's transition guidance, the author's requirements and the shared meaning-preservation principles in writing-research. Fixed templates and universal stylistic bans are not adopted.

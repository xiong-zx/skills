# Paper argument and Introduction

Read this for an outline, title, or Introduction task under doc-paper. The body of the paper and its verified results are the source of truth; an elegant Introduction cannot supply a missing result.

## Plan the argument before the prose

- Identify the precise problem and information setting, the central answer, the mechanism that makes it work, the decisive theoretical or empirical evidence, and the conditions that limit the answer.
- Check the links: why the problem matters; what the closest work establishes and leaves open; why that gap calls for this idea; which genuine obstacles the method addresses; and how each headline contribution is supported later. State a method-design obstacle through the decision the algorithm must make or the information it lacks. Distinguish that obstacle from a difficulty in proving the guarantee; put proof-specific error accounting where the analytical goal is visible. Do not invent obstacles, force one-to-one modules, or manufacture a criticism of prior work to fill a template.
- Let a literature paragraph answer the reader's current question. Explain the relevant prior operation or guarantee, then the supported difference or unresolved task addressed here. Do not make a paragraph's sole payoff that a component is familiar or that the method is not new; preserve material overlap without letting provenance interrupt the explanation of the method.
- Give every planned section a distinct job and a main takeaway. Keep an outline concise enough to expose missing links, with evidence or an explicit gap beside load-bearing claims. A provisional gap may remain visible in a working draft but not masquerade as an established submission claim.

## Title and Introduction

- Test title candidates against the actual contribution: can a reader infer the research object and leading intellectual move without an unsupported advantage claim? Choose the emphasis that matches the paper, rather than naming a familiar supporting device as the headline.
- Plan the Introduction by functions, not paragraph count: orient the reader; define the exact gap and setting; explain the central idea and why it addresses the obstacle; state supported results and their scope; and guide the reader to the evidence. Combine, reorder, or omit functions when the paper warrants it.
- Use a real running example only if it clarifies the setting or mechanism and is authorized by the author's materials. If no suitable example exists, proceed without inventing one. Keep any example consistent with the formulation, figures, and later results.
- Compare each Introduction promise with the Abstract, theorem statements, experiment captions, limitations, and section content. The claim strength and cost or feedback convention must agree. Search and verify load-bearing prior-work claims before using them; a source found only by title or snippet supports no detailed contrast.
- When asked for an outline, deliver the outline and visible gaps. When asked for prose, draft readable paragraphs through writing-research and give a brief note only for material unresolved support. Do not expose internal scaffolding, impose six paragraphs, prescribe citation counts, or insert unsupported filler.

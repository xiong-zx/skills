# Choose the depth and workflow the question needs

These are entry points, not mandatory consecutive stages. A short discussion may need one; a substantial assignment may combine them. Reuse existing context and the user's instructions before asking for more.

For a deeper assignment, briefly state the question, useful stages and a bounded first pass. If no budget is specified, choose a modest scope and disclose the limit rather than silently launching a survey or asking the user to approve every routine choice. A larger search, computation or unattended run still needs the corresponding authorization.

## Open exploration

Start from what the user wants to understand or change. Generate materially different questions with plausible reasons they matter; do not merely produce new method names. Use a suitable move from [idea-generation methods](idea-generation-methods.md).

Explain each candidate's central idea in connected language. Keep alternatives and immature ideas visible without imposing full evaluation cards, fixed counts or a winner. If the user requests only ideas, make novelty explicitly unassessed and stop there. Do not let the apparent promise of one idea silently turn open exploration into selection.

## Explain an observation

Separate the observation from its interpretation. Recover the system, conditions, measurement, variability, preprocessing and what was already inspected; mark an unverified user report as reported rather than independently reproduced.

Generate genuinely different explanations where useful, including artifacts or a simpler known account. For the important alternatives, derive predictions that differ: an intervention, controlled comparison, limiting case or mathematical consequence. Explain which outcome would favor or challenge each account and what ambiguity would remain. A test that all accounts predict cannot distinguish them.

For theory-led work with no observed anomaly, formulate a conjecture and informative special cases instead. Neither an experiment nor a causal narrative is a universal prerequisite.

## Find questions through literature

Use [prior work](prior-work-and-novelty.md), starting with the papers needed to understand the problem. For each relevant result, connect what it establishes, its conditions and the unresolved question; a small comparison may suffice.

Ask whether a claimed limitation is real, consequential and still unresolved. Follow explicit open questions forward in time; trace older or adjacent formulations backward. Sparse citations, an author's future-work paragraph or a low paper count do not establish an opportunity.

Use the evidence to generate new questions or revise the originals. Separate source-based findings from the agent's proposed extensions. Avoid a full survey of every branch when only one comparison can change the decision.

## Explore a cross-field connection

Map source objects, observations, operations, assumptions and guarantees onto the target. Classify material assumptions as satisfied, violated or unknown. Explain the structural connection with a concrete instance before suggesting an adaptation.

Distinguish a change of vocabulary, straightforward transfer and a potentially substantive extension. Compare with both relevant target-field methods and direct use of the borrowed method. The contribution might instead be a boundary, explanatory connection or impossibility result; do not force every connection into an algorithm-performance project.

## Develop or rescue one idea

Identify the weakest important link: motivation, scientific claim, missing mechanism, prior-work overlap, proof obstacle, measurement or access to resources. Fix that link rather than cosmetically polishing the description.

Possible revisions include simplifying the question, changing a substantive assumption, isolating an explanatory component, adding the missing comparison, replacing the mechanism or narrowing an unsupported claim. Preserve the original scientific goal unless the user changes it; a narrower model must be presented as a subquestion, not silently substituted for that goal.

After a material revision, recheck affected prior-work and feasibility judgments. Say what changed and why. Stop when another round produces only wording changes, repeats the same objection, needs unavailable evidence/resources or reaches the agreed budget. Do not iterate until the idea merely sounds publishable.

## Compare directions and help the user decide

Use [evaluation](evaluation-and-delegation.md). Explain the candidates before comparing them; combine duplicates by scientific content, preserving differences in assumptions or implications.

Keep the reasons for scientific value, novelty, personal fit, available first checks and remaining major difficulties visible. Distinguish a long-range question from a small near-term project; the latter is not automatically more valuable. Identify the uncertainty most likely to reverse the recommendation. Do not use a weighted total to hide a decisive constraint or weak evidence.

Recommend when asked, with conditional alternatives and the reason for each. If an important candidate should wait, say what change in evidence, resources or understanding would make it worth revisiting. If the user has not selected a direction, preserve that state.

## Expand into a research plan or proposal when requested

A user can ask for a plan for an unselected candidate; label it provisional. Choosing an idea does not by itself request a full proposal or authorize execution.

Tailor the plan to the question rather than requiring a paper-shaped template. Useful content includes the question and significance, verified closest work, intended contribution, current hypothesis/model, evidence needed and main unresolved difficulties. For a theory project, name the target claim, a tractable case and proof bottleneck. For a mechanism project, name the alternative accounts and distinguishing implications. For a method project, name the strong comparisons, metric and resource budget.

Work backward from the desired conclusion to the claims needed to support it. Distinguish:
- what can be checked with existing sources or a small calculation;
- what needs a new proof, data or implementation;
- which early finding would make later work unnecessary or change the route.

Suggest an initial feasibility check and a few meaningful milestones with their expected evidence, dependencies, resource bounds and stop/change conditions. Do not invent completion dates, results or a finished algorithm; disclose unknown effort. A negative result may motivate a boundary contribution only if it changes an important claim or expectation.

Use writing-research for the proposal's language if available. A submission-paper outline belongs to doc-paper; execution after a selected goal belongs to research-auto within the user's authorization. Do not create experiments, projects, chats or persistent teams merely because they appear in the plan.

## Resume a discussion without starting over

Read the existing topic document and relevant project state. Recover the user goal, explored and unselected candidates, decisions, reasons, known objections and new evidence. Summarize only what affects the current choice.

A failed implementation or unsuccessful proof attempt is evidence about that attempt; it does not automatically rule out the entire question. Reconsider a parked direction when its original obstacle changes, and say which obstacle changed. Do not revive a rejected topic merely because its older appealing description was retrieved.

When saving is requested, update the existing topic document with the decision reason, supporting sources, unresolved uncertainty and revisit condition. Use existing project STATUS and archive rules, without a new idea registry, per-candidate folders or per-round reports.

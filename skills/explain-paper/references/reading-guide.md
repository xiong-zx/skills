# Selective paper reading guide

Use this for a full guide or a technical passage that needs more than a summary. The user's reading priorities determine the depth; questions about the available source can proceed with a clearly stated boundary.

## Understand the argument

- Read the title, abstract, framing, main results, and conclusions to orient yourself, then inspect the method, proof, experiments, and relevant appendices needed for the actual explanation. Record which parts were read.
- Reuse an authorized local paper when supplied. For a remote source, prefer accessible primary full text and retain its version. Check extracted equations, tables, and two-column reading order against the source when ambiguous. Inspect the actual figure when a conclusion depends on curves or visual details; a caption alone cannot establish those details. Partial access permits a bounded explanation, not a claim of a complete reading.
- Recover the paper's story as a chain: motivating problem, obstacle or limitation, key move, method, claim, and supporting proof or experiment. Give the reader an outline of that argument, explaining why the major parts appear in that order and what each contributes. Listing section titles is not an explanation of the story.
- Establish the formulation before the paper's technical vocabulary. For a learning paper, make the data sources and sampling assumptions, available versus unknown information, allowed training operations, output model, loss, and evaluation quantity explicit when they matter. Distinguish the actual data-dependent procedure from population quantities, oracle sets, or idealized objects used only in the analysis. Adapt these questions to other kinds of papers rather than imposing a machine-learning template on them.
- Choose the depth of each part from the reader's progress and questions. Give an already-understood step a short bridge into the next idea. When the reader's expectation is unclear, present it as a plausible first guess. If the paper largely confirms that guess, state its main contribution without manufacturing a surprise.
- For a result that breaks an expectation, compare under the same target, assumptions, and cost measure. Identify the step that changes the answer and show it with the smallest useful derivation or counterexample. For example, a truncated gradient estimator can match an unbiased one in optimization complexity when the truncation bias is below the target accuracy. Show where the bias enters the bound and which cost determines the rate. For an assumption that makes the result possible, say what breaks without it.
- For mathematics, explain the target quantity, assumptions, mechanism, and main guarantee before subsidiary notation or algebra. Introduce symbols as they become necessary; explain the role and type of each symbol used without giving an exhaustive inventory. Unpack a central constrained set, infimum, or supremum step by step before relying on its compact formula. If a definition is motivated by an example, carry that example through the definition and compute a representative quantity so the connection is visible.
- For learning and optimization guarantees, state whether the paper assumes an exact or approximate empirical minimizer, a population optimum, an optimization oracle, or an actual finite sequence of updates. Explain whether the error being bounded comes from sampling, optimization, approximation, or their combination. An ERM-based statistical guarantee should not be described as a guarantee for a specified SGD training trajectory.
- For a proof worth discussing, give its strategy, the key lemma or inequality chain, then the meaning of the result. Distinguish a proof explanation from an independent correctness audit.
- For experiments, explain the evaluation question, comparators, metric, and conclusion. Keep an observation distinct from a causal or mechanistic interpretation.
- Scale prerequisites to the user's research background without assuming familiarity with the paper's notation or formulation. Use an analogy only when it clarifies a specific mechanism, then return to the exact setting and limits. Supply the detail needed for the first explanation to stand on its own; reserve optional technical depth for follow-ups, not missing foundations. Do not turn every concept into a beginner lesson or require a pause after every section.
- Before delivering a first explanation, check whether the reader could restate the problem's inputs and outputs, the success criterion, the key mechanism, and what the main result actually guarantees. If not, fill the missing connection before adding a personal-reading checklist. Avoid a leap from one intuitive sentence directly into dense notation or a theorem list.

## Extract takeaways

Choose the few conclusions that survive the details. Each should identify what is learned, why it follows, and where it applies. Use the main mechanism, the surprising comparison, or the decisive condition to choose these points. Leave secondary details in the reading route. Preserve negative results, boundary cases, and uncertainty when they change the takeaway. Distinguish directly reported findings, synthesis of inspected content, and interpretations; name that distinction where it changes what the user should believe, rather than tagging every sentence. Do not turn the authors' novelty claim into independently verified novelty.

## Choose the user's reading route

Keep the groups separate:

- **Core reading:** the definition, method, algorithm, theorem, proof step, figure, or experiment that reveals how the contribution works. Explain the prerequisite the reader needs and what they should understand afterward.
- **Research-provoking reading:** an inspected remark, unexpected result, failed ablation, sensitivity, assumption, proof bottleneck, negative finding, or limitation that changes interpretation or suggests a worthwhile question. State the precise tension and why it is interesting.

Use the source's section, page, equation, theorem, remark, figure, table, or appendix labels. Do not invent a locator. If numbering differs between versions, identify the version and quote a short searchable anchor. Ground each selection in inspected source text before explaining its significance. Paraphrase by default; brief original wording can resolve an ambiguity without reproducing the abstract or long passages. Give a manageable route ordered by conceptual dependency, not every page that contains technical content. If no substantial research-provoking passage was found in the inspected scope, say so rather than filling the group with generic speculation.

For a suggested research direction, distinguish an author-stated question, an inference from the paper, and an assistant proposal. Explain its source anchor and what would need to be checked; do not claim feasibility, a proof gap, an open problem, or novelty without support.

Use comparisons to the user's project only when the relevant project context is available. External papers, code, or public commentary may clarify a named question, but must be identified separately from the target paper. A first explanation does not require a broad literature search, full reproduction, or a formal referee report.
